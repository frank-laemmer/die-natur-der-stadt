# Foreword

This book explores the nature of the city, its origins, and its development through various stages of societal evolution. The following chapters examine the division of labor as a cause of urban formation, the city at its earliest stage, its breakthrough to a second stage of development, and the results of urbanization. The aim is to provide a comprehensive understanding of how cities shape and are shaped by human society.

We hope this work offers valuable insights and encourages further reflection on the role of cities in our world.

## Blurb

City and nature in their present form are the result of a long historical process. Heide Berndt's analysis shows how, throughout this process, the two extremes penetrate each other, ultimately appearing as hostile opposites. She demonstrates how the process of urbanization changes both the inner and outer nature of humans, causing them to lose their original character. The development of the city is therefore synonymous with the historical process in which the human species becomes an individual. As soon as it leaves the immediate natural context, it creates an "artificial" environment in architecture, which expresses the current stage of the confrontation between humans and nature. Contrary to the often merely aesthetic examination of architecture, this work attempts to decipher the social content of architectural forms through specific examples. By understanding the modern urban social character through its contradictory socialization, Heide Berndt is able to highlight both the positive and negative aspects of urbanization. Thus, urban life proves to be a condition for the possibility of human individuality and a humane relationship to nature; however, it also becomes clear that only a social production that is not the production of commodities, but of a humane environment, can initiate a development in which the city becomes the living center of autonomous social individuals.

## Introduction

This work attempts to understand the transformation of the individual through the process of urbanization. Today's city dwellers are different from the first inhabitants of Jericho. By demonstrating these epochal changes, the question of today's social structure and the shaping of one's own behavior by the historical process arises. Therefore, the topic of this work is linked to answering personal questions.

The connection between scientific and personal interest goes back to experiences from the student movement. The strength of its protest lay in the generality of its criticism of society and the radical questioning of one's own ways of life. Critics of the student movement have not tired of accusing it of impracticality due to this generality and radicalism.

My experience was that the failure of the movement was less due to the lack of political maturity, and more to the unresolved contradictions of this society, which became more apparent through the actions. An example is the struggle to preserve livable cities and the occupation of houses that fall victim to the ongoing process of urbanization. The search for new ways of life was largely oriented toward preserving past forms. This conservatism is no accident. The desire for non-authoritarian relationships in the future also respects the culture of ways of life developed by earlier ruling classes.

The aim of this work is to clarify and name such unclear yet strong motives, as were alive in the student movement. The most general goal of the movement was to overcome social conditions described as capitalist or bourgeois. The topic of urbanization is here directly related to the prerequisites considered as a new "stage of social development."

I am concerned with articulating a feeling of life that is urban and is deeply disturbed by the barbaric transformation of our cities. Our longing for an environment that matches this feeling cannot mean the denial of the urbanization process. The idealization of non-urban ways of life includes the suppression of the historical process, to which we owe the richness of our individuality.

Rural communes are not an alternative to the city. The mere return to pre-industrial working methods denies the diversity of human production possibilities, the richness of pleasures, and the intensification of the feeling of life, as promoted by urban coexistence. Uncomprehended urban phenomena are opposed by an equally misunderstood state of nature. To clarify the relationship between city and nature, this work gives considerable importance to the examination of ways of life that preceded all urban formation. Many elements of these ways of life shaped the earliest urban institutions. However, as they were adopted into urban conditions, they became alienated from their original meaning.

Instead of defining the city and the urban, I have tried to work out the moments of identity and transformation between city and countryside. I use the term city in a very broad sense, as it is initially applied in everyday language to the most diverse urban forms. The city is neither a cipher for an ensemble of architectural facts nor merely a collection of people in a limited space. Rather, it is a social entity in which human relationships have undergone profound changes.

The goal of this work is to show the transformation of "outer" nature as well as "inner" nature through the development of the concept of the city. There is no "return to nature," at most a persistence in barbarism. Non-urban, nature-near ways of life, as prevailed in the Paleolithic, are not the natural paradise the civilized person may dream of. The way out of urban barbarism can only consist in making the urban environment a natural, i.e., an environment "close" to today's people. The ruthless exploitation of nature, which currently characterizes the relationship to the environment, not only destroys its own character but also makes it threatening. The poisoning of air and water are signs of this, as is the general aesthetic disfigurement, which gives it the character of something unnatural. It is important to recognize the shaping of the environment essentially as a social problem, whose technical-ecological solution is only possible with the transformation of social relationships.

To anticipate a result: the city must be understood as a transitional institution. It is the link between the cityless prehistory of humans and today's "total urbanization." Its effect was twofold: within its territory, it created conditions through which human behavior took on civilized traits; and it subjected the countryside to its production methods, changing vast areas in their form and character. Making essential stages of this dual transformation process vivid will be the main part of this work. The transformation of the tribe member, who received his identity through the totem, into a person with individual consciousness, is to be shown as well as the emergence of certain city and architectural forms. It should become clear that urbanization is a process that began long before the industrialization of society. My work is therefore not a history of the city, but a presentation of its social conditions of origin up to "total urbanization." It is also an attempt to bring back to mind buried elements of human motives and designs for environmental shaping, as they were still alive in pre-bourgeois urban forms, since such knowledge can help vague wishes for other ways of life become more concrete ideas and best prevent the resigned "return to nature."
