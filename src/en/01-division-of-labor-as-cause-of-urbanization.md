# Division of Labor as the Cause of Urbanization

The city is the result and symbol of the transformation of nature by human labor. Its forms are determined by the organization of labor. Marx and Engels especially emphasized this. They saw the city as the result of the first fundamental social division of labor. As stated in "The German Ideology": "The division of labor within a nation initially leads to the separation of industrial and commercial from agricultural labor, and thus to the separation of town and country and the opposition of their interests." [^1-1] It is immediately added: "The various stages of the division of labor are just as many forms of property." The opposition between town and country is also understood as an expression of the "greatest division of material and intellectual labor." [^1-2]

  [^1-1]: Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, The German Ideology, in: MEW (Marx-Engels Works, ed. by the Institute for Marxism-Leninism at the Central Committee of the SED), Berlin (GDR), Vol. 3, p. 22
  [^1-2]: ibid., p. 50

The separation of physical from intellectual labor found its visible expression in the city in profane and sacred architecture. A key function of the city, namely to be a center of power, was always evident in its architectural structure in pre-industrial times.

The separation of intellectual from physical labor and the resulting "opposition of interests" primarily involves social power relations: the exploitation of those who only perform physical labor and the exemption from drudgery for those who perform intellectual labor. The great inventions of the first urban high cultures in Mesopotamia and Egypt—writing, arithmetic, and geometry, which greatly increased labor productivity—consolidated the exercise of power. The productive functions of the city are therefore inseparable from power. This is the ambivalent character of the urban. The negative side of this ambivalence is expressed in the disparaging judgment that cities are artificial.

But the land surrounding the cities was also not left in its natural state, but was shaped by artificial changes. The irrigation cultures of the ancient Orient make this clear. Although the city was always the starting point for such transformations, the negative aspects of this process are not to be blamed on the city as such, but on the social conditions that determine the relationship between city and country as a whole. Marx's derivation of the categories of city and country from the division of labor immediately points to the social character of this relationship. It was also shaped by the opposition between rulers and the ruled. Division of labor meant not only a technically sensible distribution of work, but also established the opposition of different professions and classes, which moved within the dimension of power and exploitation. All of previous civilization is marked by this. Similar to the statement of a natural law, Marx said: "With the moment when civilization begins, production begins to be built on the opposition of professions, estates, classes, and finally on the opposition between accumulated and immediate labor. Without opposition, there is no progress; that is the law that civilization has followed to this day." [^1-3] The city is the clearest expression of the contradictions that characterize the progress of civilization.

The view of the city as the result of social division of labor and the fixation of property leads, in order to get a historical idea, to the question of how the division of labor that already existed was incorporated into the forms of cities. Of course, there were forms of division of labor long before the emergence of the first city-like settlements. The first forms of division of labor, it is said in "The German Ideology," were "natural"; they were based on the differences between men and women, young and old. [^1-4] Even before the emergence of the first cities, the natural division of labor had developed into social division of labor. The first "full specialist" is the archaic medicine man and magician. [^1-5] The urban king inherited his social characteristics.

  [^1-3]: Marx, Karl, The Poverty of Philosophy, in: MEW 4, ibid., p. 91/92
  [^1-4]: MEW 3, ibid., p. 31
  [^1-5]: Schmoller, Gustav, The Social Question. Class Formation, Workers' Question, Class Struggle, Munich/Leipzig 1918, p. 10/11; Coon, Carleton S., The History of Man, From the First Human to Primitive Culture and Beyond, London 1955, p. 105

The division of labor, so crucial for the emergence and growth of cities, was, however, limited in archaic societies.

The clan, the community of people of the same totem, which was obviously the most primitive form of human cohesion, was based on equal cooperation among its members. Apart from the naturally imposed forms of division of labor, the clan knew no division of labor, because its principle of life was based on equal work performance by its members. This cooperation did not arise from higher insight, but on the basis of "rigid conformity," a "mechanical" solidarity, as Durkheim put it. [^1-6]

The self-sufficiency of clans or clan communities that had grown into tribes had to be overcome as the greatest obstacle to overarching social organization and corresponding population concentration before cities could arise. Even the totem associations or family and kinship groups that had become sedentary, like the ancient gentes, produced only for the needs of their members or their household (= oikos); economy was synonymous with oikos-economy (= economy). The needs of the oikos were assessable and limited. As long as economic activity was tied to the oikos, the development of the division of labor was limited and cities could not grow beyond a certain size. "Primary city foundations" had a double origin: the residence of the lord with the associated castle on one side and the simple dwellings of ordinary people on the other. This obvious division is based on two different causes of city formation. While, for example, Gordon Childe in his explanation of the archaic "urban revolution" particularly emphasized that with the transition to sedentism, the population increased because food was more plentiful thanks to productive agricultural techniques, Marx emphasized another aspect. In his view, cities arose to protect communal property, to provide military security for the tribe's possessions. The castle was usually used as a refuge by all in times of need. If the lord was able to persuade his people to settle together, then the city arose from various village or house communities in one stroke. The Greeks called this "synoikismos." [^1-7] Synoikismos was by no means just a symbolic process: its visible result was the construction of the city. [^1-8]

  [^1-6]: see also G. Childe, for whom this cooperation did not differ significantly from the sense of belonging of a pack of wolves or a herd of sheep. Childe, Gordon, The Urban Revolution, in: Town Planning Review (Liverpool) April 1950, p. 7
  [^1-7]: Kuhn, Emil, On the Origin of the Cities of the Ancients. Constitution of the Komen and Synoikismos, Leipzig 1878, p. 154

As the word synoikismos suggests, the city was "a community of all oikoi," i.e., clan communities engaged in agriculture. The field that such a family usually received by lot from the city's land distribution was inalienable property. Transfers of property could only take place in classical Athens if "the claims arising from kinship" were set aside.

If the tribal assembly was the precursor of the city, then the village corresponded to the settlement of the clan; development did not go from village to city, [^1-9] but village and city were equivalent, albeit different, forms of settlement. The "nature of the city" draws from the nature of the tribal association, the stimulation it provides for the individual. The city is therefore not only quantitatively, by the size of the settlement, different from the village, but also qualitatively, in terms of its origin and its significance for human coexistence. For the ancients, it was the epitome of civilization, which stood out positively from the barbarism of cityless societies. [^1-10]

As the settlement of the tribe, it contained the elements of the tribal assembly, the festive design of the gathering of the many, which historically determined the nature of the city. The tribal association was identical with great festivities, with the lifting of taboos that regulated the life of the clan. This is a point to which Durkheim paid attention, without, however, making a connection to the periodic city festivals of archaic urban cultures. Durkheim took from reports of tribal gatherings of Australian aborigines the exaltation of the individual as an outstanding feature of these annual events. The presence of the many apparently meant a physical stimulation, which was released in wild shouting and gesticulation. There was an "emotional chaos," which was transformed into "regulated tumult" through rhythmic dancing. [^1-11]

During the tribal festival, the usual rules of life, especially the incest taboos, were relaxed, making these gatherings the highlights of life and reaffirming the connection of the otherwise isolated clans. [^1-12]

The nomadic life of the Australian aborigines, who hunted for meager food in small groups, lacked such uplifting moments as the cults of the tribal assembly offered. According to Durkheim, totemism developed from the tribal festivities. [^1-13b] Even the civilized Greeks did not do without the special highlight of a periodic great tribal assembly and established the Olympic Games for this purpose. Olympia was a Greek national sanctuary; it was a city, even if without residential houses. [^1-14]

Karl Kerényi also saw a close connection between the festivities of archaic societies and their religion. He described the ancient religions as "festival religions." The festival is "transformed time," "high" time. [^1-15] "We must not grasp festivity only where it already appears elevated to the timeless heights of art, but where it has not yet completely left time behind and emerges from it, as it were; where time itself, distinguished by festivity, together with its experiential content, becomes tangible."

The festival was the means of assuring social cohesion through a special social act. Dance and music were used to promote the special sense of time and to evoke the feeling of festivity, an indispensable part of the festival. The festival was not only a cheerful, but also a serious and uplifting affair. It served collective memory, thus tradition and the preservation of past forms, but also allowed for unusual spontaneity through the temporary suspension of taboos.

The city as the embodiment of the community is based on two initially opposing social phenomena: its character of power results from the division of intellectual and physical labor, while its positive, community-building function has its origin in those festivities in which taboos were lifted. However, this element of the "nature of the city" was overlaid as its sphere of power expanded. Through Christianity and the bourgeois development of the city since the Middle Ages, the still periodically occurring festivals were given a different meaning. With the beginning of modern times and the dissolution of autonomous cities, national festivals shifted to the representative events of the court, and the people were excluded from them.

  [^1-13b]: ibid., p. 313
  [^1-14]: Wycherley, R.E., How the Greeks Built Cities, London (2) 1962 (1st ed. 1949), p. 91/92
  [^1-15]: Kerényi, Karl, Ancient Religion, Wiesbaden 1971, p. 46

In the festival, it was no longer the community that was embodied, but only the ruling class. The secularization of power by the bourgeois revolution in France did not bring the old city festivals back to the people. The festivals could no longer fulfill their earliest function of shaping and making time tangible, as the new production methods required a new time order based on purely quantitative principles, and this time order is expressed not in the festival, but in precise timekeepers, modern clocks.

The power relationship that the city entails thus becomes more apparent. In the consciousness of many people, city life therefore appears as "unnatural" and is rejected. It is supposed to be replaced by healthier, more nature-oriented ways of life. But this longing for nature, which is commercially exploited in many ways by health food stores, makes one forget that the nature of the city includes festivity and the stimulation of the human spirit, which is bound to the physical presence of the many. It is also forgotten that the setting that the festival finally received in courtly representation, baroque landscape architecture, the special atmosphere that belonged to the festival, could be part of everyday urban living.

## 1. City and Country as Opposing Unity

In Marxist theory, references to the city are found only in relation to the country as its opposite. This opposition is essentially explained as a manifestation of the dominance of intellectual over physical labor, so that the concept of the city does not possess the concreteness that the familiar image of "city-country" might suggest. By deriving the nature of the city from the nature of property relations and the social organization of labor, Marx left aside those tangible features peculiar to the city or the city-country opposition, which are otherwise cited by other social scientists attempting to define the city by constant characteristics. Thus, he did not concern himself with the classification of special urban features, such as market, castle, temple, fortification, etc., but primarily tried to present the social mechanisms that drive social development, and thus also the development of the city. Nevertheless, Marx assigned considerable importance to the process of urbanization within his theory of society. As stated in "Capital": "The basis of all developed and exchange-mediated division of labor is the separation of town and country. One can say that the entire economic history of society is summarized in the movement of this opposition, which we will not go into further here." [^1-17a]

  [^1-17a]: Marx, Karl, Capital, Berlin 1961, Vol. I, p. 369

From the use of the terms, city and country always appear as a unity, albeit an opposing unity, but never isolated from each other. It is striking that in Marxist theory, whenever the city is mentioned, the country is also mentioned. Overall, a power relationship is meant. However, the term country also tempts one to associate it mainly with geographical features. But the geographical images of this opposition only capture their objectified manifestations and easily obscure the view of the transformation that this relationship as a whole has undergone. Because Marx did not further address the historical development of the city-country opposition, but rather turned to more general conditions and results of the division of labor, some Marxist theorists have had difficulty recognizing the social transformation of this relationship. [^1-18] They also say surprisingly little about the modern geographical manifestation of this opposition, which has necessarily changed due to the general urbanization in industrialized countries. Marx's conscious neglect of the development of the city-country relationship can only be explained by his view that this relationship in modern bourgeois society is the result of the forms of property and class relations set by capital. [^1-19]

At the forefront, Marx wanted to present the functioning of bourgeois society and did not consider it necessary "to write the real history of the relations of production in order to develop the laws of bourgeois economics." [^1-20] Therefore, he devoted his attention to the more general categories for capturing the mechanics of bourgeois society, such as exchange and commodity production, the development of property relations, but not the city. He assumed that the correct presentation of social mechanisms or laws "always leads back to first equations, which point to a past lying behind the system." [^1-21]

Incidentally, he made such a historical excursus on the manifestations of elementary property, communal property, and in doing so came to speak of the nature of cities. [^1-22]

Since the nature of the city is to be understood from its historical constitution, Marx's historical sketches offer important methodological clues. Particularly important is that Marx always understood the development of city and country as a unity. For while food for the townspeople was produced in the countryside and urban institutions emerged from "rural" ones, rural production and lifestyles also changed according to the development of the city. This complicated, mutually conditioning relationship can best be illustrated by the very different interpretations of early historians when they explain the peculiarities of cities under oriental despotism.

Early historians such as Karl August Wittfogel, in his work on the "natural causes of economic history," were unable to do justice to this relationship, as they equated the category of land or nature too one-sidedly with features such as climate, soil conditions, and the presence of plants and animals. On the basis of this rather "geographical" than dialectical materialism, Wittfogel tried to demonstrate the determining effect of these natural factors on the forms of archaic societies, mainly the "hydraulic societies" of the Near East. "The development of the social productive forces, centrally that of their material core: technology, is determined by the structure of the respective natural elements." [^1-23] He explained this in a strange polemic against human free will: "No free will, not even for the technically, the productively active human. Whether he transforms the landscape on his journey through the mountains, or after each advance blasted into the rocks faces new tasks and new prospects. His path must adapt to the 'passive' structure of the mountains. And if he takes his path through the air, it is not arbitrarily, but in accordance with new, deeper, more complex structural laws of nature." [^1-24] In the course of his studies, Wittfogel came closer to Marx's view when he stated that in more developed societies, the determining power of mere natural conditions diminishes greatly. He correctly reported that Marx attributed less influence to "objective" nature—climate, geography—than to "subjective" nature—race, ethnology—on social institutions. [^1-25]

Marx also assumed that the "subjective" nature of humans changes according to the transformation of "objective" nature, and that from a certain stage of social development, an "average level" of temperaments emerges; thus Wittfogel finally wondered: "Is not everything now social, the product of historical development?" only to immediately deny it, as this did not fit his vulgar materialist concept. Wittfogel's mechanical approach to materialism leads to characteristic one-sidedness in his interpretation of history. Thus, he could show that Oriental despotism based its power on the bureaucratically controlled irrigation system, and that the land-intensive agriculture of these countries (Egypt, Mesopotamia) made the profitable use of slaves unnecessary, meaning that the hot climate and desert-like land had a strong impact on social development. But exactly this could often be shown just as well by the "bourgeois" theorists he disparaged, such as Max Weber. Wittfogel cannot show why the Babylonians and Egyptians, along with their highly rational production facilities—the irrigation systems—also expended great collective effort not only to maintain water management, but also in monumental temple and tomb construction. To dismiss the religious institutions of these societies as mere "superstructure," which they could have reasonably done without, would be to misunderstand their real significance. A dialectical view of history must do justice to the unity of religion and the corresponding mode of production. There was no separate economic rationality alongside the religious institutions of pre-industrial societies, such as the Sumerian temple economy, which determined the then-prevailing mode of production in its particular form. The separation of economic and religious morality only occurs with the advance of bourgeois elements within economic processes, especially with the spread of money circulation. If one does not recognize the connection between religion and the mode of production in pre-industrial societies, one loses the sense for ways of life that are incompatible with bourgeois thinking and feeling. Wittfogel also overlooked that the transformation of the land always took place in the name of power and was therefore completely shaped by the language and ideas of the then-prevailing authority. The oriental city was considered the property of a god or several gods; to the consciousness of the time, it was not the natural forces in their immediacy that appeared as active forces in the production process, but rather the deities, who often represented natural forces but were distinct from the actual forces of nature. The ability to perceive natural forces without mythical disguises is an achievement of bourgeois society and the scientific perspectives developed within it. Wittfogel missed this.

Conversely, non-Marxist historians, such as Henri Frankfort, the eminent American orientalist, took the religious truth of archaic epochs too literally and got no further in explaining the temple economy than the ancients themselves, who saw the temple as the property of a god ruling in absence. Frankfort objected to Gordon Childe's remark that with the Mesopotamian cities, the opposition between city and country arose for the first time, arguing that "the great opposition between city and country, between rural and urban life" is a product of the industrial revolution and did not exist in earlier epochs of history. Frankfort is certainly right to emphasize that one must not transfer this opposition "in the form in which we know it" to other historical circumstances. In his dispute with Childe, however, he assumed that this opposition basically exists only in one form, namely "as we know it." [^1-26] Thus, he did not see the transformation of the relationship between city and country. For this reason, his understanding of ancient urban institutions, especially the temple economy, did not go much beyond what the ancients themselves said. Despite his extremely rich historical knowledge, Frankfort had a rigid, unhistorical view of social institutions.

In contrast, Childe's interpretation of the Mesopotamian temple provided the crucial key to understanding both its functioning and its origins. Not only did the oldest cities arise together with the opposition between city and country; but the "urban revolution" of which Childe speaks in this context also solved the settlement problems that the Neolithic economy could not cope with as populations increased. [^1-27]

The institutions of the early cities were still entirely dominated by "rural" features; the urban temple communities initially represented nothing other than the "vastly enlarged version of the patriarchal household from barbaric times." [^1-28] The Sumerian temple was a great oikos that provided for all its members, thus producing collectively, just as the old clan community had done.

  [^1-26]: Frankfort, Henri, The Birth of Civilisation in the Near East, London (2) 1954 (1st ed. 1951), p. 57/58
  [^1-27]: Childe, Gordon, Stages of Culture. From Prehistory to Antiquity, Stuttgart (2) 1955 (Engl. 1941), p. 84
  [^1-28]: ibid., p. 118

For this reason, Moortgat questioned whether the temple settlements could be regarded as cities at all, since they were more comparable to a medieval monastic estate. [^1-29] The temple authority supervised the irrigation systems and allocated each family a food ration; in return, the families had to deliver fixed shares of the harvest or other services to the temple. The temple community is therefore to be regarded as an institution of agrarian origin, which bears great similarity to the Germanic mark communities or is comparable to the Indian village communities. Anna Schneider drew this conclusion from her study of the "Sumerian temple city." In her view, private economic relations within this system are of more recent origin. Above all, the private household of the temple overseer, the patesi, who often rose to become city king, is to be understood as a secession from the originally communal property. In this, she expressly contradicted Max Weber, who saw the temple economy as a foundation of the city king. [^1-30] Anna Schneider went a step further in her interpretation. Temple economy and the Sumerian pantheon, in her opinion, originated from totemism, the most archaic form of human coexistence. She had noticed, for example, that the goddess Bau was often depicted with an emblem showing a chicken. These "divine attributes" are probably the "remnants of a totemistic cult." "If, therefore, the gods are named as owners of the land, this should be understood as meaning that originally the land was the property of the people's community. With increasing culture and the inclusion of outsiders into the economic association, the old kinship views and the religious ideas associated with them must have faded and become independent." [^1-31] This interpretation of the urban temple economy in Sumer fits easily into Marx's definition of property, according to which tribal or communal property is its oldest form. The particular achievement of Anna Schneider, as in a certain way also that of Gordon Childe, lies in linking these forms of the first urban economic and religious practices with those of totemism. Although the researchers of totemistic societies had also recognized the persistence of old customs and ideas in historically developed societies, it was precisely this connection between the development of forms of property and the "development of the spirit" that had not been systematically accomplished.

  [^1-30]: Schneider, Anna, The Sumerian Temple City, in: Plenge, Staatswissenschaftliche Beiträge, Issue IV: The Beginnings of Cultural Economy, Essen 1920, p. 29
  [^1-31]: ibid., p. 98

In the explanations of materialist historians, there is usually a lack of adequate understanding of the significance of the totem, hence the use of the term "primitive society" instead of totemistic social form. In other researchers, totemism is described in detail, but its logic in relation to economic possibilities is mostly neglected.

From the connection between totemism and the earliest urban institutions, the specific form-determination of the social labor of that time can be understood. The forms of communal life that originated from totemism determined the forms of economic activity within the ancient urban cultures. One manifestation was that a large part of the harvest was delivered to the temple to materially secure the unity of the community in the cults dominated by the priestly class. That this unity was based on a power relationship is probably not to be seen as an "urban original sin," but, in all likelihood, was set at the dark beginning of human history. The rule of intellectual labor over physical labor, which more than mere violence forms the core of domination, did not arise only with the opposition of city and country, but from this stage of social division of labor developed into the social relationship of different human groups: ruling class versus immediate producers.

The dominance of the city, inherent in the relationship between city and country, was expressed in the fact that the city subjugated the country and also changed the landscape according to urban ideas. This power relationship can take very different geographical forms. Henri Pirenne, the great Belgian historian of medieval urbanism, gave a very vivid description of the difference between pre-industrial and modern forms of this relationship: "In our time, cities are not separated from the countryside: there are factories in villages, and on the other hand, part of the urban population lives in the rural surroundings and returns there every evening after work. In the Middle Ages, however, it was quite different, for city and country were completely separated. The city, even materially, was completely cut off from the countryside by its ditches and walls; it was also legally a different world. As soon as one entered its ground through one of the city gates, one was in a territory of different law: a difference that one nowadays only experiences when passing from one state to another." [^1-32] By emphasizing the different legal relationships between city and country, Pirenne pointed to the social content of this opposition.
